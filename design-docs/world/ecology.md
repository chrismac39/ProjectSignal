# Project Signal — Ecology Design

## 1. Purpose

Ecology in Project Signal is part of the strategic world state.

The ecosystem is not decorative wildlife, a static resource layer, or a collection of neutral creatures placed around the map for visual interest.

Ecology exists to:

* make the world feel independently alive,
* create changing strategic conditions,
* produce ambiguous signals,
* support Plastai biomass acquisition and biological progression,
* create hazards and opportunities for Vanguard,
* react to terrain, climate, weather, fire, water, and conflict,
* influence movement and logistics,
* create migration and regional change,
* generate emergent stories,
* prevent the map from becoming a solved board.

**Established principle:** the ecosystem is an independent living system that both factions must read, exploit, survive, and adapt to.

---

## 2. Ecology Design Philosophy

Project Signal requires strategically believable ecology, not a complete scientific ecosystem simulation.

The system favors:

* coherent habitats,
* persistent populations,
* regional ecological productivity,
* migration,
* predator–prey relationships where strategically useful,
* environmental response,
* interaction with weather,
* interaction with geography,
* interaction with world-state modifiers,
* readable strategic consequences.

The ecology should be deep because systems interact, not because every biological process is individually modeled.

Avoid:

* simulating biology for its own sake,
* requiring every individual animal to possess a complete life simulation,
* static wildlife resource nodes,
* random creature placement without habitat logic,
* wildlife that exists only to feed the Plastai,
* ecosystems that immediately return to equilibrium after disruption,
* fixed species coordinates that can simply be memorized.

**Hard rule:** ecology is simulated only to the depth required to produce meaningful strategic behavior.

---

## 3. A Living World Independent of the Players

The ecosystem continues to function whether either player interacts with it or not.

Wildlife may:

* migrate,
* feed,
* reproduce at an abstract population level,
* concentrate around water,
* seek productive habitat,
* flee danger,
* respond to predators,
* respond to fire,
* respond to weather,
* compete,
* follow seasonal patterns,
* shift habitat,
* respond to changes in vegetation,
* react to conflict and infrastructure.

The environment can therefore produce meaningful events without either faction causing them.

A major herd may leave a valley because of drought.

Predators may follow migrating prey into a new region.

A wildfire may empty an ecosystem before either player arrives.

Heavy rainfall may transform a previously marginal wetland into an important concentration of life.

This independent behavior is critical to the information model.

Not every unusual ecological event indicates enemy activity.

---

## 4. Baseline Biosphere

The baseline world supports an unusually rich and productive biosphere.

Its broad climatic identity is alpine and continental, but its biological productivity and viable animal size range substantially exceed those of modern Earth environments with comparable geography.

The biosphere is characterized by:

* high ecological diversity,
* extensive terrestrial productivity,
* productive freshwater and wetland systems,
* abundant large herbivores,
* substantial predator populations,
* broad terrestrial, aquatic, amphibious, aerial, and burrowing niches,
* much greater megafaunal abundance than modern Earth,
* viable organisms across an enormous range of body sizes.

The ecological feel takes broad inspiration from prehistoric Earth in its richness, biomass, and abundance of very large animals.

It is not intended to recreate any specific geological period.

Elevated atmospheric oxygen and other planetary conditions support the setting's highly productive biosphere, but the design does not depend on a literal recreation of Earth paleobiology.

The world simply possesses atmospheric, physiological, ecological, and evolutionary conditions capable of supporting unusually large quantities of life and unusually large organisms.

**Hard rule:** megafauna are part of the normal biosphere. They are not exceptional boss monsters.

## 5. Species and Ecological Roles

Species are designed primarily around ecological roles.

Potential roles include:

* large herbivores,
* small herbivores,
* grazers,
* browsers,
* predators,
* scavengers,
* aquatic species,
* amphibious species,
* aerial species,
* burrowing species,
* herd animals,
* territorial animals,
* solitary megafauna,
* migratory species,
* wetland specialists,
* alpine specialists,
* forest species,
* open-country species.

The ecology system defines the environment these species inhabit.

It does not require an exhaustive bestiary before the ecological simulation can function.

Individual species should reinforce ecological structure rather than exist primarily as collectible monsters or isolated gameplay gimmicks.

---

## 6. Population Scale

Wildlife exists in numbers large enough to matter strategically.

Project Signal does not use the common game abstraction where an immense wilderness contains a handful of decorative animals.

Regions may support:

* very large herds,
* multiple interacting populations,
* migration waves,
* extensive predator territories,
* concentrated breeding areas,
* dense aquatic populations,
* major seasonal aggregations,
* solitary animals whose individual size makes them strategically relevant.

The exact representation can vary by population.

A herd containing thousands of animals does not require thousands of individually simulated agents at all times.

Population scale should be preserved conceptually even when simulation is abstracted.

---

## 7. Habitat

Species distribution follows habitat logic.

Relevant variables may include:

* biome,
* elevation,
* water availability,
* temperature,
* vegetation,
* terrain,
* season,
* prey availability,
* shelter,
* snow,
* recent weather,
* fire history,
* world-state modifiers.

Habitats establish suitability rather than fixed spawn coordinates.

A player who understands the ecosystem may reasonably infer where a species is likely to occur without knowing its exact current location.

This distinction is important.

Players should learn ecological logic rather than memorize resource coordinates.

---

## 8. Regional Ecological Productivity

Different regions support different amounts and kinds of biological productivity.

Productivity may depend on:

* rainfall,
* surface water,
* vegetation,
* temperature,
* soil and moisture conditions,
* season,
* elevation,
* biome,
* recent wildfire,
* drought,
* flooding,
* recent weather history.

Highly productive regions naturally attract:

* herbivores,
* predators,
* scavengers,
* breeding populations,
* migrating animals,
* Plastai attention.

Low-productivity regions remain ecologically meaningful but support different densities and survival strategies.

Productivity is not permanently fixed.

A familiar valley can be highly productive in one campaign and substantially less productive in another because of:

* global world state,
* weather history,
* procedural ecological variation,
* fire,
* drought,
* flooding,
* population pressure.

This supports replayability without discarding geographic logic.

---

## 9. Vegetation and Biomass

Vegetation is represented strategically rather than plant by plant.

Vegetation influences:

* herbivore carrying capacity,
* habitat suitability,
* Plastai biomass opportunity,
* wildfire fuel,
* concealment,
* movement,
* erosion and soil state where relevant,
* long-term ecological recovery.

Biomass represents living ecological capacity.

It is not a layer of generic green resource points.

The amount of useful biological material in a region emerges from the region's actual ecology.

A lush wetland, dense forest, migrating herd, productive grassland, and aquatic concentration may all provide biomass differently.

---

## 10. Biomass as a Plastai Resource

The Plastai depend upon the biosphere for biological input.

Potential sources include:

* wildlife,
* vegetation where appropriate,
* aquatic life,
* carcasses,
* other usable organic material.

Harvested biological material contributes to:

* biomass conversion,
* nutrient production,
* biological construction,
* brood development,
* other faction systems defined elsewhere.

The amount of biomass available in a region depends on:

* ecological productivity,
* population density,
* species composition,
* migration,
* prior exploitation,
* weather,
* fire,
* season,
* habitat.

Biomass is not simply money growing on the map.

Heavy exploitation changes what remains afterward.

The ecology therefore determines:

* where biomass exists,
* how concentrated it is,
* how accessible it is,
* how quickly it can recover,
* what consequences follow from harvesting it.

---

## 11. Ecological Depletion

Heavy exploitation has ecological consequences.

Possible consequences include:

* herd decline,
* disappearance of local populations,
* changed migration,
* predator decline or relocation,
* reduced local biomass,
* reduced future productivity,
* scarcity,
* habitat disruption,
* long recovery periods,
* ecological collapse in extreme cases.

The Plastai therefore interact with a resource base that can be damaged.

This does not mean every harvesting decision should require conservation micromanagement.

The purpose of depletion is to create strategic relationships with regions and populations.

A player may choose between:

* aggressively exploiting a herd now,
* preserving it as a recurring resource,
* driving it toward another area,
* abandoning an overused region,
* competing for a more productive ecosystem elsewhere.

**Hard rule:** biomass is not infinitely renewable without consequence.

---

## 12. Recovery and Regrowth

Ecological systems can recover.

Recovery may depend on:

* time,
* season,
* weather,
* remaining population,
* habitat quality,
* migration,
* vegetation recovery,
* water,
* absence of continued disturbance.

Recovery must occur on campaign-relevant timescales where appropriate.

This does not require detailed population genetics, individual mating simulation, or plant succession modeling.

Different disturbances can recover differently.

A temporarily displaced herd may return quickly.

A heavily depleted population may require much longer.

A burned grassland may eventually become highly productive again.

A region that loses both habitat and population may recover slowly or develop a different ecological state.

---

## 13. Migration

Migration is both ecological behavior and a strategic system.

Wildlife may migrate in response to:

* season,
* water,
* forage,
* weather,
* snow,
* predators,
* fire,
* drought,
* flooding,
* human infrastructure,
* Plastai activity,
* combat,
* habitat depletion.

Migration changes the geography of biological resources.

A valuable herd is not guaranteed to remain where it began.

Migration can:

* relocate biomass,
* create signals,
* create uncertainty,
* generate temporary opportunities,
* alter predator distributions,
* interfere with Vanguard infrastructure,
* expose or conceal faction movement,
* concentrate wildlife in contested areas.

Migration is therefore not cosmetic background animation.

It is one of the mechanisms that prevents the ecological map from becoming static.

---

## 14. Migration Corridors

Geography shapes migration.

Likely corridors include:

* valleys,
* river systems,
* mountain passes,
* plains,
* wetlands,
* lake systems,
* shorelines where appropriate,
* other terrain that naturally channels movement.

Migration corridors can overlap with:

* Vanguard roads,
* railways,
* bridges,
* industrial sites,
* Plastai biomass access,
* aquifer emergence areas,
* strategic chokepoints,
* productive ecological regions.

This overlap creates natural conflict.

A valley may simultaneously be:

* the easiest place for a Vanguard transport corridor,
* the natural route of a major herd,
* a productive Plastai harvesting region,
* a route predators follow.

No special scripted objective is required for that valley to become strategically important.

---

## 15. Predator–Prey Dynamics

Predator–prey relationships exist at the level required to create strategic ecological behavior.

Predators may influence:

* herd movement,
* regional danger,
* carcass availability,
* migration,
* signals,
* population pressure,
* Plastai harvesting opportunities,
* Vanguard operations.

Predators do not require a complete trophic simulation.

The system only needs enough ecological interaction for consequences to be coherent.

If prey moves, predators may follow.

If major prey populations collapse, predator activity may change.

If a region becomes unusually productive, both herbivores and predators may become more common.

**Hard rule:** do not construct a detailed food-web simulator unless the resulting behavior creates meaningful gameplay.

---

## 16. Megafauna

Very large native animals are ordinary components of this biosphere.

Megafauna may:

* form large herds,
* migrate across enormous distances,
* consume large amounts of vegetation,
* create major biomass opportunities,
* support predator populations,
* damage or disrupt infrastructure,
* cross roads and rail corridors,
* alter terrain locally,
* disturb wetlands,
* generate strong signals,
* become ecological landmarks.

Their strategic significance emerges from:

* size,
* population,
* behavior,
* movement,
* ecological role.

They are not boss monsters placed for combat encounters.

The world supports enormous possible body-size ranges under every major climatic state.

Hot, cold, wet, or dry world conditions can change:

* phenotype,
* distribution,
* behavior,
* habitat preference,
* migration,
* relative abundance.

They do not eliminate megafaunal scale from the biosphere.

---

## 17. Dangerous Wildlife

Native animals can threaten both factions.

Possible consequences include:

* attacks on exposed Vanguard personnel,
* damage to light infrastructure,
* disruption of convoys,
* territorial obstruction,
* predation on other wildlife,
* conflict with Plastai organisms,
* interference with work sites,
* dangerous concentrations around water or breeding areas.

Wildlife behaves according to ecological motives.

Animals should not become generic hostile mobs that attack anything entering an aggro radius.

Aggressive behavior may emerge from:

* predation,
* defense,
* territoriality,
* panic,
* competition,
* protection of offspring,
* displacement,
* unusual environmental stress.

---

## 18. Wildlife and the Vanguard

The Vanguard experiences the ecosystem as a technologically advanced but biologically unfamiliar force.

Potential interactions include:

* collision hazards,
* attacks on personnel,
* migration blocking or crossing routes,
* infrastructure damage,
* biological uncertainty,
* reconnaissance contacts,
* confusing ecological signals,
* industrial disturbance of wildlife,
* dangerous regions that must be avoided or cleared.

The Vanguard possesses advanced sensors but is not a dedicated scientific expedition.

Its forces are primarily concerned with extraction, logistics, infrastructure, reconnaissance, and defense.

It therefore does not automatically understand:

* what every species is,
* which animals are dangerous,
* what behavior means,
* why wildlife is moving,
* whether an ecological signal indicates enemy action.

This uncertainty supports the broader Project Signal information model.

---

## 19. Wildlife and the Plastai

The Plastai are native participants in the biosphere.

Potential interactions include:

* hunting,
* harvesting,
* observation,
* ecological manipulation,
* biological assimilation opportunities,
* competition,
* predation,
* exploitation of migration,
* directing activity toward productive regions,
* preserving useful ecological resources.

Native wildlife does not automatically obey the Plastai.

The Plastai are not a hive mind commanding the planet's animals.

They may understand native biology more intuitively than Vanguard, but wildlife remains independent.

Animals can:

* avoid them,
* attack them,
* compete with them,
* flee from them,
* be exploited by them,
* interfere with their plans.

**Hard rule:** the Plastai are part of the ecosystem, not magical controllers of it.

---

## 20. Ecological Knowledge

The two factions understand the ecosystem differently.

The Plastai may possess:

* deeper lived familiarity with native biological mechanisms,
* intuitive recognition of many ecological relationships,
* stronger understanding of native behavior and usable biological traits.

The Vanguard may possess:

* advanced sensors,
* aerial observation,
* remote monitoring,
* powerful analytical tools,
* incomplete biological interpretation.

Neither faction necessarily has perfect ecological knowledge.

A Vanguard sensor may detect a moving herd accurately while misunderstanding why it is moving.

The Plastai may recognize ecological behavior more readily while possessing less comprehensive remote observation.

The exact information asymmetry should reinforce Project Signal's broader signal philosophy.

---

## 21. Ecology as Signal

Ecological change produces information.

Potential ecological signals include:

* herd movement,
* sudden wildlife absence,
* predator displacement,
* carcass concentrations,
* disturbed vegetation,
* unusual migration,
* mass movement from a valley,
* aquatic disturbance,
* changes in feeding patterns,
* concentrations around shrinking water sources.

These may indicate:

* weather,
* wildfire,
* predators,
* seasonal migration,
* Vanguard activity,
* Plastai activity,
* resource depletion,
* environmental disturbance,
* unrelated natural ecological change.

Ecological signals should frequently be ambiguous.

A player detects consequences.

The player must interpret their cause.

---

## 22. Plausible Non-Combat Interpretation

Ecological observations do not automatically indicate enemy action.

A herd leaving a region might indicate:

* approaching weather,
* predators,
* wildfire,
* drought,
* flooding,
* water scarcity,
* normal migration,
* industrial noise,
* Vanguard movement,
* Plastai harvesting,
* combat.

The possibility of innocent ecological explanations is fundamental to the signal loop.

Players should rarely be able to treat one ecological observation as perfect confirmation of enemy activity.

Repeated observations, context, and other signals increase confidence.

---

## 23. Ecology and Weather

The ecology system consumes the environmental state produced by `world/weather.md`.

It does not duplicate the weather simulation.

Weather can affect:

* water availability,
* vegetation,
* migration,
* breeding where relevant,
* herd concentration,
* fire,
* habitat,
* mortality,
* predator behavior,
* regional productivity.

The effect depends on existing world state.

A thunderstorm in a wet region may:

* improve water availability,
* temporarily disrupt animals,
* create minimal fire risk.

The same thunderstorm after prolonged drought may:

* ignite wildfire,
* scatter herds,
* kill wildlife,
* destroy vegetation,
* drastically reduce expected biomass.

**Established principle:** weather interacts with ecology through environmental state rather than predetermined event outcomes.

---

## 24. Ecology and Fire

Wildfire can transform ecosystems.

Immediate consequences may include:

* animal mortality,
* mass movement,
* destroyed vegetation,
* lost biomass,
* smoke,
* predator displacement,
* habitat loss.

Longer-term consequences may include:

* changed migration,
* new vegetation,
* open terrain,
* altered forage,
* recovering productivity,
* new habitat structure.

Fire is therefore both destructive and ecologically transformative.

It should not simply apply permanent damage to a region.

Its long-term effect depends on:

* severity,
* biome,
* season,
* rainfall,
* post-fire weather,
* surviving populations.

---

## 25. Ecology and Water

Water is one of the strongest drivers of ecological concentration.

Water availability influences:

* vegetation,
* migration,
* herd density,
* breeding behavior where relevant,
* predators,
* aquatic populations,
* wetlands,
* regional productivity,
* Plastai activity.

Lakes, rivers, springs, wetlands, and other reliable water sources naturally become centers of biological activity.

Changing water conditions can therefore reshape strategic ecology.

Drought may empty one region while concentrating wildlife around remaining water.

Flooding may temporarily create productive wet habitat elsewhere.

Water should influence ecological geography without turning every water source into an identical resource node.

---

## 26. Ecology and Seasonal Change

Seasonal change influences:

* migration,
* vegetation,
* water availability,
* snow cover,
* regional productivity,
* predator behavior,
* breeding where strategically relevant,
* habitat accessibility.

The baseline world possesses strong alpine and continental seasonality.

Major world modifiers change the expression of the seasons without eliminating ecological variety.

A hot world can still support:

* snow,
* alpine environments,
* cold-season migrations,
* high-elevation specialists.

A cold world can still support:

* wetlands,
* forests,
* productive valleys,
* warm-season growth,
* grasslands,
* very large animals.

Seasonal systems should remain strategically legible without requiring detailed calendars for every species.

---

## 27. Ecology and the Aquifer System

The Plastai aquifer system intersects with surface ecology but does not perfectly mirror it.

Possible points of interaction include:

* springs,
* wetlands,
* lakes,
* aquatic ecosystems,
* productive valleys,
* biomass concentration,
* brood emergence,
* nutrient collection.

Surface biological resources can feed Plastai nutrient flows into the underground network.

However:

* not every productive ecological region is an aquifer hub,
* not every aquifer access point is ecologically rich,
* productive regions can exist far from useful emergence points.

This mismatch creates strategic decisions about movement, harvesting, storage, and access.

---

## 28. Species Distribution as Session Variability

Exact species distribution and density vary between campaigns.

Players may understand:

* what habitats a region can support,
* what ecological roles are likely,
* where water or productivity suggests wildlife concentration.

They should not automatically know:

* which exact species are present,
* exact herd positions,
* exact population sizes,
* current migration routes,
* current predator concentrations.

Major world-state modifiers can also alter phenotype expression and ecological distribution.

A related lineage may appear differently under hot, cold, wet, or dry conditions while retaining a recognizable ecological relationship.

This supports a core Project Signal rule:

**Players learn ecological logic rather than memorizing ecological coordinates.**

---

## 29. Hidden Ecological Conditions

Many local ecological conditions are naturally unknown at campaign start.

Examples include:

* unusually large herds,
* predator concentrations,
* rare species,
* altered migration,
* high-productivity areas,
* ecological imbalance,
* recently depleted populations,
* unusual aquatic concentrations.

These conditions do not require separate hidden global modifiers.

They can emerge from procedural session variation and ongoing simulation.

Players discover them through:

* reconnaissance,
* observation,
* signals,
* movement,
* interaction with the environment.

---

## 30. World-State Modifier Interaction

Ecology participates directly in the session variability system.

The strongest world-state modifiers are non-votable global conditions.

Examples include broad climatic states such as:

* hotter world,
* colder world,
* wetter world,
* drier world.

These modifiers can substantially alter:

* biome boundaries,
* water distribution,
* vegetation,
* productivity,
* migration,
* phenotype prevalence,
* snow,
* drought,
* wildfire risk.

They do not eliminate the world's underlying ecological diversity.

A hot world is not desert everywhere.

A cold world is not snow everywhere.

Both retain broad habitat variety and enormous viable body-size ranges.

Public player votes are weaker shared-world modifiers.

They are not faction-specific bonuses.

Possible ecological consequences include conditions such as:

* unusually abundant megafauna,
* altered predator prevalence,
* expanded marshland,
* unusually productive aquatic systems,
* other environmental changes with meaningful tradeoffs.

A player can vote for a world state that appears advantageous to the opponent.

For example, a condition associated with greater accessible surface hydrocarbons may also produce wetter or more biologically productive landscapes useful to the Plastai.

Public modifiers should therefore represent tradeoff packages rather than simple positive or negative effects.

**Hard rules:**

* Public modifiers change the shared world.
* They do not belong to the player who selected them.
* Both factions can exploit their consequences.
* Public modifiers are not direct faction buffs.
* Ecological effects should include advantages, disadvantages, or second-order consequences rather than a single numerical bonus.

The hidden vote is a faction/subfaction choice and is outside the scope of this ecology document.

---

## 31. Ecology and Infrastructure

Vanguard infrastructure changes animal behavior where strategically relevant.

Potential effects include:

* roads crossing migration routes,
* rail corridors,
* bridges,
* noise,
* lighting,
* barriers,
* industrial disturbance,
* local pollution where appropriate,
* altered access to water.

These consequences should only be modeled when they create meaningful strategic behavior.

The design does not require a complete environmental impact simulator.

A railway through a migration corridor matters if it causes:

* collisions,
* delays,
* herd rerouting,
* predator movement,
* ecological signals,
* infrastructure vulnerability.

If an ecological consequence has no meaningful gameplay effect, it need not be simulated.

---

## 32. Ecology and Conflict

Combat disturbs ecosystems.

Potential consequences include:

* explosions,
* fire,
* fleeing wildlife,
* carcasses,
* destroyed habitat,
* altered migration,
* predator attraction or displacement,
* long-lived population changes.

Conflict can therefore leave ecological evidence.

A major battle may remain detectable afterward because:

* wildlife avoids the region,
* vegetation is damaged,
* scavengers concentrate,
* migration changes.

These ecological consequences can themselves become signals.

---

## 33. Ecology and Deception

Natural ecological behavior can provide cover for faction activity.

Examples include:

* Plastai movement occurring alongside a major migration,
* wildlife displacement being mistaken for military movement,
* Vanguard operations concealed by environmental disturbance,
* faction activity hidden by storms,
* movement timed around predictable herd behavior.

The system does not require explicit fake-animal mechanics.

Deception emerges because several different causes can produce similar observable consequences.

---

## 34. Ecological Events

Larger ecological events can emerge from system state.

Potential examples include:

* major migration,
* population boom,
* herd collapse,
* predator expansion,
* regional scarcity,
* fire-driven mass movement,
* drought concentration,
* aquatic concentration,
* recovery of previously damaged habitat.

These events should usually arise from interacting ecological conditions.

They are not arbitrary scripted events inserted solely to create drama.

Their causes may be simplified internally, but their outcomes should remain coherent with the state of the world.

---

## 35. Ecology and Time Compression

Ecology continues to change during accelerated time.

The simulation should support:

* gradual migration,
* changing populations,
* vegetation recovery,
* ecological depletion,
* weather-driven movement,
* regional productivity change.

The game does not need to update every animal individually during long periods of compressed time.

Ecological systems can resolve at higher abstraction while preserving strategically meaningful outcomes.

Time compression should surface major events such as:

* a major herd entering an important region,
* a valuable population departing,
* a serious ecological collapse,
* fire destroying a biomass source,
* migration threatening infrastructure.

Ordinary ecological activity should not constantly interrupt the player.

---

## 36. Simulation Abstraction

Ecology is simulated at the lowest level necessary to produce the intended strategic behavior.

Possible abstractions include:

* regional populations,
* herds,
* territories,
* migration groups,
* regional biomass,
* habitat suitability,
* ecological productivity.

Individual entities can exist where tactically or visually useful.

A herd may therefore be represented as:

* a strategic population during long-range simulation,
* a moving group while migrating,
* visible individual animals when encountered locally.

The design remains free to mix population-level and entity-level simulation.

**Hard rule:** the entire biosphere does not require individual-agent simulation.

Abstraction must preserve consequences rather than invisible detail.

---

## 37. Strategic Consequences

Ecology creates decisions such as:

* whether to harvest a herd now or preserve it,
* whether to contest a productive valley,
* whether to build infrastructure through a migration corridor,
* whether wildlife movement indicates enemy activity,
* whether a region can recover from exploitation,
* whether wildfire has temporarily or fundamentally altered an area,
* whether to follow a migrating resource,
* whether to protect or deny biomass,
* whether ecological concentration makes a region worth exposing forces to danger.

Ecology therefore becomes part of strategy rather than background.

---

## 38. Emergent Narrative

The ecology system should produce stories through interaction rather than scripted events.

Examples include:

* a major herd unexpectedly entering a contested valley,
* predators scattering exposed Vanguard personnel,
* the Plastai overharvesting a productive region and later facing scarcity,
* wildfire destroying a biomass source the Plastai intended to exploit,
* migration interfering with Vanguard rail construction,
* both factions misinterpreting natural wildlife movement as hostile activity,
* prolonged rain transforming a minor wetland into a strategically valuable ecosystem,
* drought concentrating enormous numbers of animals around one remaining water source,
* a conflict scattering wildlife whose later movement reveals activity elsewhere.

These stories emerge from:

* geography,
* climate,
* weather,
* animal behavior,
* infrastructure,
* resource use,
* conflict.

---

## 39. What the Ecology System Must Never Become

The ecology system must not become:

* decorative wildlife,
* static resource nodes,
* a zoo collection game,
* a scientific ecosystem simulator for its own sake,
* a full individual-agent simulation of every creature,
* random hostile mobs,
* ecology that exists only for Plastai harvesting,
* perfectly predictable species placement,
* fixed herd coordinates,
* infinite renewable biomass without consequences,
* an ecology where weather has no effect,
* an ecology independent of terrain,
* a system where migration is cosmetic,
* a system where every ecological disturbance indicates enemy activity,
* a system where the Plastai automatically control native wildlife,
* a system where megafauna exist only as boss encounters,
* a system where world modifiers erase ecological diversity,
* a system requiring excessive player micromanagement,
* a simulation whose complexity exists without producing strategic behavior.

The ecosystem should feel deep because its systems interact.

It should not require every ecological process to be explicitly simulated.

The following ecology questions remain unresolved:

* Exact population abstraction level.
* Exact representation of large herds.
* Exact balance between population-level and individual-agent simulation.
* Exact depth of predator–prey interaction.
* Exact biomass conversion and depletion rates.
* Exact ecological recovery rates.
* Exact seasonal migration logic.
* Exact aquatic ecology scope.
* Exact consequences of extreme overharvesting.
* Exact species discovery mechanics.
* Exact ecological knowledge asymmetry between factions.
* Exact number and granularity of native species required for the initial playable ecology.
* Exact rules for climate-responsive phenotype variants.
* Exact mapping between ecological productivity and usable Plastai biomass.

These questions should be resolved only when required by gameplay or implementation.

---

## 41. Ecology Design Test

Any future ecology mechanic should be evaluated against the following questions:

* Does this make the world feel independently alive?
* Does it produce meaningful strategic change?
* Does geography matter?
* Does climate matter?
* Does weather matter?
* Does prior ecological state matter?
* Does it create signals?
* Can those signals have more than one plausible interpretation?
* Does it support Plastai biomass without becoming a simple resource-node system?
* Does exploitation produce meaningful consequences?
* Can the ecosystem recover where appropriate?
* Does it affect Vanguard operations?
* Does it support migration or regional change?
* Does it support session variability?
* Does it encourage players to learn ecological logic instead of map coordinates?
* Does it preserve enormous ecological and body-size diversity across world states?
